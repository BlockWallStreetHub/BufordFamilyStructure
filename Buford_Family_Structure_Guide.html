SECTION 1 The Big Picture

Why do families build a “structure” at all?

Most families own things: a house, a car, maybe a business, some savings, maybe land or investments. When a family owns just a few things, owning them in your own name works fine. But as a family grows, so do the problems: lawsuits, taxes, arguments, and what happens when someone dies. A family structure is a set of legal “containers” that hold what the family owns, protect it, and make sure it is run by clear rules instead of by guessing.

The Four Goals

1. ProtectKeep what the family built safe from lawsuits, creditors, bad decisions, and mistakes.
2. GrowUse money in smart, organized ways so it makes more money over time.
3. Pass OnMove wealth and wisdom to the next generations smoothly, without court fights.
4. GiveUse part of the family’s success to help the community and live out the family’s values.

Meet the Five Pieces

This guide covers five pieces. Think of them like players on a team. Each one has a position, and none of them wins alone.

Nonprofit / FoundationAn organization that uses its money for a public good instead of for owners’ profit.The giving arm

Problems a Structure Helps Solve

  • Lawsuits. If someone slips on the steps of a rental house you own personally, they can sue you, and your savings and your home may be at risk.
  • Probate. When a person dies, assets in their own name usually go through probate, a court process that can be slow, public, and expensive.
  • Family fights. Without written rules, relatives argue about who gets what, who is in charge, and who has to pay.
  • Mixing everything together. When business money and personal money share one account, it is hard to track, hard to protect, and hard to explain to the IRS.
  • Loss of knowledge. If only one person knows where everything is and how it works, the family is stuck when that person is gone.
  • Taxes. Smart planning can legally lower what a family owes. We will talk about this carefully.
Important: avoidance vs. evasion

Tax avoidance means using the rules the law gives you to pay the lowest tax you legally owe. That is allowed. Tax evasion means hiding money or lying to pay less. That is a crime. A good structure is built on avoidance, and it is always honest and well documented.

Buford example (make-believe)

Imagine Paul Buford, the grandfather of the family, owns a rental house, a small landscaping company, and a brokerage account, all in his own name. A worker is injured on the landscaping job and sues. Because everything is in his name, the lawsuit can reach the rental house and the savings, too. If Paul passes away, all three go through probate, and his kids may disagree about who runs what, while his grandkids are left out of the planning. Everything you learn in this guide is about making that story turn out differently.

SECTION 2 The Family Trust

The vault with the rulebook

A trust is a legal arrangement where one person gives property to be held and managed by another person for the benefit of someone else. It is written down in a document called the trust agreement (or “declaration of trust”). The document is the rulebook. It says what is in the trust, who is in charge, who benefits, and exactly how and when the money can be used.

The Cast of Characters

RoleAlso calledWhat they do
GrantorSettlor, trustorCreates the trust and puts property into it.
TrusteeManages the trust property and follows the rulebook. This is a fiduciary job, which means they must put the family’s interests first.
BeneficiaryThe person (or group, or charity) who benefits from the trust.
Successor trusteeBackupSteps in when the first trustee dies, retires, or cannot serve.
Trust protectorOptionalA trusted person with power to fix the trust over time, for example to replace a trustee or update the rules when laws change.

The property inside the trust is called the trust property, the principal, or the corpus (Latin for “body”).

In plain words

Picture a safe-deposit box. The grantor puts valuables in and writes the instructions. The trustee holds the key and follows those instructions. The beneficiaries are the people the box is for. The key holder cannot just do whatever they want, because the written instructions and the law keep them in check.

Two Main Types

What a Trust Does for a Family

  • Avoids probate. Assets owned by the trust are not “in the dead person’s name” anymore, so they can usually pass to the family quickly and privately, without the court.
  • Sets the rules. The grantor can say things like: “Money for college and a first home. Distributions at age 25, 30, and 35. Not for gambling.” This helps protect young people from receiving too much, too soon.
  • Protects from outside problems. A well-built irrevocable trust, with a spendthrift clause, can keep a beneficiary’s creditors (or an ex-spouse) from grabbing the trust’s money before it is paid out.
  • Plans for incapacity. If the grantor becomes too sick to manage money, the successor trustee steps in without needing a court to appoint a guardian.
  • Lasts across generations. A trust does not die, get old, or move away. Some states allow “dynasty trusts” that can last for many generations.
  • Keeps things private. A will becomes a public court record. A trust normally stays private.
The step people forget: “funding” the trust

A trust is just an empty box until you put things in it. That means changing the title on the house deed, the bank account, or the company ownership so that it says “The Buford Family Trust” as the owner. A beautifully written trust that owns nothing protects nothing. Your attorney will walk you through this.

The Trustee’s Duties

Being a trustee is a serious job. A trustee must:

  • Follow the trust document exactly.
  • Act in the best interest of the beneficiaries (duty of loyalty).
  • Invest carefully, like a smart and cautious person would (prudence).
  • Keep good records and share reports with beneficiaries.
  • Treat beneficiaries fairly and never use trust money for themselves.

A family member can be trustee, or the family can choose a bank or professional trustee, or use a team of both (co-trustees). Choosing the trustee is one of the most important decisions in the whole plan.

What a Trust Is Not

  • It is not a magic tax-free box. Trusts are taxed, and trust tax brackets are “compressed,” which means a trust hits the highest tax rate at a much lower income than a person does. Good planning decides who should be taxed on what.
  • It is not an unbreakable shield. Courts can undo transfers made to dodge a lawsuit that already exists.
  • It is not “set it and forget it.” Trusts need to be reviewed when the family changes, such as births, deaths, marriages, divorces, or new businesses.

Buford example (make-believe)

Paul Buford, the grandfather, creates The Buford Family Trust. He names himself trustee while he is healthy and names his daughter as successor trustee. He moves his rental house and his ownership in the landscaping company into the trust. His grandchildren are beneficiaries. The rulebook says: “Pay for college. Help with a first home. Give a share of each grandchild’s portion at 25, 30, and 35.” When Paul passes, no probate is needed, and the rulebook keeps running.

SECTION 3 The LLC

The shield between the business and your personal life

LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. It is a business that the law treats as its own “person,” separate from the people who own it. The owners are called members. The key word is “limited”: if the LLC gets sued or owes money, the members’ personal belongings (home, car, personal savings) are generally not on the line. The most they can usually lose is what they put into the LLC.

In plain words

Think of an LLC as a fireproof room. If a fire starts in the room (a lawsuit or a debt), the walls are built to keep it from spreading to the rest of your house. But those walls only work if you built them right and keep them in good shape.

How an LLC Is Set Up and Run

  1. Pick a state and a name. The name must be unique and usually ends with “LLC.”
  2. File “Articles of Organization” with the state and pay a filing fee.
  3. Name a registered agent. This is the person or service that receives legal papers for the LLC.
  4. Write an Operating Agreement. This is the rulebook for the LLC: who owns what percent, who makes decisions, how profits are split, and what happens if someone wants out or passes away.
  5. Get an EIN (Employer Identification Number) from the IRS. It works like a Social Security number for the LLC.
  6. Open a separate bank account in the LLC’s name.
  7. Stay in compliance. Many states require yearly reports and fees.

Member-Managed or Manager-Managed?

  • Member-managed: The owners run the day-to-day business themselves.
  • Manager-managed: The owners pick one or more managers to run things. The managers may or may not be owners. This is common for family LLCs where some relatives want to share ownership but not make daily decisions.

How an LLC Is Taxed (By Default)

LLCs are called pass-through entities. The LLC itself usually does not pay federal income tax. Instead, the profit “passes through” to the owners and shows up on their personal tax returns.

  • An LLC with one member is normally treated as a “disregarded entity,” meaning the IRS looks right through it to the owner.
  • An LLC with two or more members is normally taxed like a partnership.
  • An LLC can choose to be taxed as a corporation (S-corp or C-corp) if that works better. A CPA helps decide.

Keeping the Shield Strong

Courts can ignore the LLC’s protection if the owners treat it like a fake. This is called “piercing the veil.” To keep the shield, do these things:

  • Never mix money. Do not pay your grocery bill from the LLC account or deposit LLC income into your personal account.
  • Put everything in writing. Contracts, loans between the LLC and its owners, and big decisions.
  • Follow your Operating Agreement and the state’s rules.
  • Sign as the LLC. For example: “Buford Rentals LLC, by Jane Buford, Manager.”
  • Keep enough money or insurance in the LLC to handle normal risks. An LLC with no money and no insurance looks like a sham.
Limits of the shield

An LLC does not protect you from your own wrongdoing, such as fraud, or from a personal guarantee you signed on a loan. It also does not replace insurance. A smart family uses both.

Why Families Love LLCs

  • Liability protection for each business or property.
  • Flexible ownership. Ownership is divided into “membership interests” which can be shared among relatives in any percentage.
  • Charging-order protection in many states. If one member is sued personally, the creditor often can only get a “charging order,” which means they may collect that member’s share of distributions but cannot take over the LLC or force it to sell its assets. This varies a lot by state.
  • Easy to hold in a trust. A trust can own LLC membership interests, which links the pieces together.
  • One LLC per risk. Putting each rental property or business in its own LLC means trouble in one will not drag down the others.

The Bufords create Buford Rentals LLC for the rental house and Buford Landscaping LLC for the lawn business. Each has its own bank account, its own insurance, and its own Operating Agreement. When a worker gets hurt on a landscaping job and sues, the claim goes after Buford Landscaping LLC. The rental house sits in a different LLC, so it is not part of that fight.

SECTION 4 The Holding Company

The parent that owns the children

A holding company does not usually sell products or serve customers. Its main job is to own things: other companies, real estate, investments, patents, trademarks, and more. The companies it owns are called subsidiaries (or “subs”). The holding company is the parent.

A holding company is not a separate kind of legal entity. It is a role. Most family holding companies are set up as LLCs, so you might see a name like “Buford Holdings LLC.”

In plain words

Think of a family tree for businesses. At the top is the parent (the holding company). Underneath are the children (the operating companies). The parent owns the children, but each child has its own bank account, its own debts, and its own lawsuits. If one child gets in trouble, the parent and the other children are protected.

Holding Company vs. Operating Company

Holding companyOperating company
Main jobOwns assets and other companiesRuns a real business: sells, builds, serves customers
EmployeesFew or noneMany
Risk of lawsuitsLow (it doesn’t deal with customers)Higher (daily business creates risk)
Buford exampleBuford Holdings LLCBuford Landscaping LLC

Why Use a Holding Company?

  • Firewalls between businesses. A lawsuit against one subsidiary generally stays in that subsidiary. The holding company and the other subs are protected.
  • One place for control. Instead of the family owning ten different things in ten different ways, the family’s trust owns one holding company, and that company owns everything else. Decisions are easier to coordinate.
  • Easier to sell or add pieces. To sell the landscaping business, the holding company sells that one subsidiary. The rest of the family business keeps running.
  • A central “bank” for the family. Profits from successful subs can move up to the holding company, which can then invest them or lend them to a new venture. This is the “closed loop” idea we will see in the Getty section.
  • Protecting valuable assets. The risky, busy business sits below. The valuable stuff (property, investments, brand names, cash) sits above, in the holding company, away from daily risk.
  • Ownership planning. Relatives can own shares in the holding company, which is cleaner than splitting up each separate business.

How Money Moves Inside the Group

Money moves between the parent and the subsidiaries in a few ways. All of them must be real, written down, and fair:

  • Distributions: A sub sends profit up to its owner (the holding company).
  • Capital contributions: The holding company puts money into a sub to help it grow.
  • Intercompany loans: One company lends to another with a written agreement, a fair interest rate, and a repayment plan.
  • Service or management fees: If the holding company (or the family office) truly does work for a sub, such as bookkeeping or management, the sub can pay a fair fee under a written agreement.
  • Rent: If the holding company owns the building and the sub uses it, the sub pays fair rent.
Rules you cannot skip

These payments must be for real services at fair prices. If the family invents fees just to move money around or to lower taxes, the IRS or a court can ignore them, and the shield between the companies can crack. Every company should keep its own bank account, books, and records. Mixing money between companies without paperwork is the fastest way to lose the protection.

Possible Drawbacks

  • More paperwork and cost. More companies mean more filings, more bank accounts, and more bookkeeping.
  • State fees. Some states charge yearly fees for each LLC. This can add up.
  • Needs discipline. Someone has to keep the records straight, and that’s a big reason families hire a family office or an accountant.
  • Lender rules. Banks sometimes ask for personal guarantees even when a company is the borrower.

Buford example (make-believe)

The Buford Family Trust owns 100% of Buford Holdings LLC. Holdings owns three subs: Buford Rentals LLC (real estate), Buford Landscaping LLC (a business with employees and equipment), and Buford Investments LLC (stocks and other investments). If Landscaping ever gets sued, the rentals and the investments are walled off from that lawsuit.

SECTION 5 The Nonprofit & Family Foundation

The giving arm of the family

A nonprofit is an organization that exists for a public or community purpose instead of for the owners to get rich. Nonprofits can make money, but the money must be used for the mission. No one owns a nonprofit the way someone owns an LLC. It is controlled by a board of directors.

Most nonprofits are set up as nonprofit corporations at the state level. To be tax-exempt, they also apply to the IRS. The most common status is 501(c)(3), named after the section of the tax code. A 501(c)(3) must be organized for purposes like charity, education, religion, science, or helping the community.

In plain words

A nonprofit is like a community garden: the garden belongs to the community’s mission, not to any one person. The people running it are caretakers. They can get paid a fair wage for real work, but they can’t take the garden’s money home as private profit.

Two Big Types of 501(c)(3)

Public charityPrivate foundation
Where money comes fromMany donors and the publicUsually one family or one source
Typical activityRuns programs: classes, food pantry, mentoringMostly gives grants to other charities (some also run programs)
RulesFewer restrictionsStricter rules, extra taxes, and required yearly giving
Who controls itA board, often broader than familyThe family often fills the board

The Family Foundation Rules

Many wealthy families start a private family foundation. The family puts money or assets into it, usually gets a tax deduction (within limits), and the foundation invests the money and gives it away over time. Foundations come with strict rules:

  • Required giving: A private foundation generally must give away about 5% of its assets each year for charitable purposes.
  • No self-dealing: The foundation cannot do business deals with the family (such as buying a family member’s house, lending to relatives, or paying unfair salaries). Violations bring heavy penalties.
  • No private benefit (“inurement”): No one gets rich from the foundation’s money. Family members can be paid a reasonable amount for real work.
  • Excise tax: Private foundations usually pay a small tax on their investment income.
  • Public reports: They file a yearly tax form (Form 990-PF) that anyone can look up. So foundations are not private the way trusts are.
  • No political campaigning and only limited lobbying.
An easier option: the donor-advised fund

If the family only wants to give, and doesn’t want all the paperwork of running a foundation, it can open a donor-advised fund (DAF) at a community foundation or a financial company. The family donates, gets the tax deduction right away, and recommends where the money goes over time. It’s simpler, though the family has less control.

How a Nonprofit Fits with Family Wealth

  • Values in action. It lets the family give in an organized way that matches what they believe in: education, health, housing, youth, faith, or business training.
  • Tax benefits. Gifts to a qualified charity can lower the donor’s income tax and, in big estates, estate tax. The rules have limits and details, so a CPA should guide this.
  • Teaches the next generation. Kids and grandkids can serve on the board and learn to review requests, make decisions, and see the impact of giving.
  • Builds the family name. A family known for helping its community earns trust, which helps the family’s businesses, too.
  • Gives the wealth a purpose. Wealth that only serves itself gets boring and risky. Wealth that serves others feels meaningful.

Nonprofit Do’s and Don’ts

  • Do keep it completely separate from the family’s for-profit businesses: separate bank account, books, and board meetings with notes.
  • Do follow the mission written in your paperwork.
  • Don’t use nonprofit money for personal expenses.
  • Don’t promise donors something you can’t deliver.
  • Don’t call yourself tax-exempt until the IRS actually approves your 501(c)(3) application.

SECTION 6 The Family Office

The coach, the manager, and the front office in one

A family office is a private team (or company) that manages a family’s wealth and affairs. It does for the family what a big company’s management does for a business: keeps the books, watches investments, plans taxes, handles legal and insurance paperwork, and keeps the plan on track.

In plain words

If the trust is the vault and the LLCs are the shields, the family office is the team that runs the whole building. It knows where everything is, pays the bills, talks to the lawyers and accountants, and reports back to the family.

What a Family Office Can Do

AreaExamples
InvestmentsChoosing and watching investments, tracking performance, rebalancing.
Accounting & bookkeepingKeeping the books for every entity, paying bills, producing reports.
Tax planningWorking with CPAs, making sure returns are filed on time and correctly.
Legal & complianceTracking state filings, annual reports, trust updates, and company deadlines.
Insurance & riskMaking sure homes, businesses, vehicles, and people are properly insured.

Lifestyle & conciergeTravel, property management, security, and special projects (mostly for very large families).

Types of Family Offices

  • Single-Family Office (SFO): Serves one family only. Gives total privacy and control, but can be expensive. Often used by families with very large wealth.
  • Multi-Family Office (MFO): Serves several families, so the costs are shared. Families get professional help without running their own staff.
  • Virtual or “mini” family office: A smaller family builds a simple version using a trusted family member who acts as the organizer, plus an outside CPA, attorney, and financial advisor who work together. This is often the best starting point for families who are still building.
Good to know

People often think family offices are only for billionaires. The big, fully staffed ones usually are. But the ideas of a family office (central records, regular meetings, a written plan, a team of advisors) can help any family, at any size, to start small and grow into it.

Family Governance: The “Human” Part

Money problems in families are often people problems. A good family office also builds governance, which means the way the family makes decisions together. Common tools include:

  • Family mission statement: A short statement of why the family is doing all this and what it stands for.
  • Family constitution (or charter): A written set of rules for how decisions are made, who can join the business, how disagreements are settled, and how members can exit.
  • Family council: A group that meets regularly, perhaps quarterly, to talk about money, the businesses, and the foundation.
  • Next-generation training: Teaching young family members about money, business, and giving before they receive anything.
  • Conflict plan: A process (like a mediator) for settling disagreements before they become feuds.

How the Family Office Is Built Legally

A family office is usually its own company, often an LLC (for example, “Buford Family Office LLC”). It can be owned by the family trust or the holding company. It is paid by the entities it serves, through fair, written service agreements. This keeps the paperwork clean and the money flow honest.

Watch out for
  • Cost. A staff is expensive. Don’t build a big office before the family’s assets can support it.
  • Trust and conflicts. Whoever manages the money has a lot of power. Use checks and balances, such as two signatures, an outside CPA, and regular reports.
  • Investment rules. Managing other people’s money may require licenses. A single family managing only its own money generally has more flexibility, but your attorney should confirm.

SECTION 7 How Each Piece Helps the Others Grow

The Getty “money spider” and the Buford loop

The word appreciate has two meanings, and both fit here. To appreciate something is to value it. And when an asset appreciates, it grows in value. The five pieces of a family structure do both: each one needs the others, and each one helps the others grow.

The Getty Scene

From the Getty story on screen

In the scene, oil tycoon J. Paul Getty explains to his grandson how the family trust works. Instead of taking profit out and spending it, the trust keeps reinvesting across the entire chain of the business: oil fields, shipping, refineries, gas stations, and even workforce housing for the workers. Money earned at one stage goes straight into the next stage. It is a closed loop that feeds itself.

He compares it to a “money spider.” The web keeps getting bigger and stronger, but on paper the operation shows no profit at the end of the year. Because it records no profit, he explains, it pays little or no tax.

What the scene gets right
  • Vertical integration. That’s the big word for owning each step of a supply chain. If you own the oil field, the tankers, and the gas stations, you don’t pay other companies’ profit margins in the middle. You keep that value inside your own family’s web.
  • Reinvestment. A web grows when you keep adding to it. Money that stays in the system keeps working, and it can grow much faster than money that is spent each year.
  • Long-term thinking. The trust is meant to outlive any one person, so it can think in decades, not months.
  • One owner, many pieces. The trust sits on top as the long-term owner, and the company pieces below do the work.
Reality check: the part that is TV, not tax law

A television drama makes things sound simple. In real life, a family cannot make taxes disappear just by calling everything a “loss.” Here is what is true:

  • Taxes are based on real income minus real, allowed expenses. Money you spend on genuine business costs (equipment, wages, repairs, interest, depreciation) can lower taxable profit. That part is legal.
  • Putting profit back into a growing business does not by itself erase taxes. Buying an asset is often not a deduction. It is spread out over time (depreciation), or it is taxed when the asset is sold.
  • Losses that exist only on paper, or that come from a business with no real profit motive, can be denied by the IRS. The rules for this are called hobby-loss and passive-activity rules.
  • Defer is not delete. Taxes pushed into the future usually come due later, when assets are sold or money is paid out.
  • Inventing fake losses is tax evasion, a crime.

So take away the structure idea (reinvest, own the whole chain, think long-term), not the idea that taxes vanish. A CPA turns that idea into something safe.

The Loop in Seven Steps

  1. The Trust owns Buford Holdings LLC, so the family’s wealth sits under one long-lasting owner.
  2. Holdings owns the operating LLCs, so each business has its own shield.
  3. Buford Repairs LLC fixes the houses owned by Rentals LLC and does jobs for Landscaping. It is paid fair, written rates, so family money stays in the family web.
  4. The LLCs send profits up to Holdings.
  5. Holdings reinvests: it buys another rental, funds a new business idea, or builds a cash reserve for hard times.
  6. Holdings sends a planned share to the Foundation, which gives to the community.
  7. The Family Office tracks every dollar, files every form, and reports to the family council, so the loop stays honest and legal.

A Lesson from the Real Getty Family

The real Getty family also shows what can go wrong. Their trusts and companies were powerful, but over the years relatives disagreed about control and about selling the family oil company. Court fights, rivalries, and unhappy family members became part of the story. The lesson: legal structure holds the money, but only family unity, honesty, and good communication hold the family. That is why the Family Office’s governance tools (the constitution, the council, next-gen training) are just as important as the trusts and LLCs.

SECTION 8 Together vs. Alone

Why a family does better building as one, even in business

The Bundle of Sticks

An old fable tells of a father whose sons kept quarreling. He handed each son one stick and told him to break it. Each son snapped it easily. Then he tied a bundle of sticks together and asked each son to break the bundle. None of them could. His point: alone you break easily; together you are strong.

A family structure is the rope that ties the bundle. But the rope only works if everyone agrees to be in the bundle.

Seven Reasons the Team Wins

  1. Strength in numbers. Pooled money, pooled skills, and pooled connections.
  2. Shared protection. Legal walls around each piece, and a safety net for family members who have hard times.
  3. Lower costs through scale. One accountant, one insurance review, one legal team for the whole family.
  4. Clear plans for the future. Written rules for who leads, who inherits, and how people may leave.
  5. Teaching the next generation. Young people grow into responsible owners instead of surprised heirs.
  6. A bigger shared purpose. Family members see themselves as builders of something bigger than themselves.
  7. Staying power. Individuals pass away, but the trust, the holding company, and the foundation keep going.
Why this matters most for families who are building

Families who are starting from the ground up often don’t have a large pile of money. They do have something powerful: each other. Combining effort, trust, and organization is how a family turns small beginnings into a base that can last for generations, in a way that no one person could do alone.

The Honest Part: What Togetherness Requires

Working together is not automatic. Families can fall apart over money. These are the things that keep a family together:

  • Clear roles. Who is the trustee? Who is the manager? Who runs the foundation? Write it down.
  • Everything in writing. Operating agreements, loan notes, and the trust all need to be on paper, not only “understood.”
  • Fair rules for everyone. No favorites. The same rules apply to the oldest and the youngest.
  • An exit plan. A buy-sell agreement explains how someone who wants out can be paid fairly, without breaking up the family business.
  • Regular, honest meetings. Share the numbers. Secrets create suspicion.
  • A way to settle fights. Mediation first. Court last.
  • Respect for different goals. Not everyone wants to run a business. Structure can let some be owners and others be investors or givers.
  • Separate what’s family and what’s business. Business decisions follow business rules. Family love shouldn’t become an excuse to ignore them.
When “alone” can be the right answer

Being together is a choice, not a trap. If relatives truly can’t trust each other, or someone wants a completely different path, a good structure includes a fair way to separate. A buy-sell agreement and clear ownership shares make a peaceful split possible. That fairness is part of what makes it safe to join.

Questions for the Buford Family Meeting

Use these to start a real conversation. There are no wrong answers yet. The goal is to find out what the family wants before the attorney draws anything up.

  1. What do we already own (property, businesses, investments, savings, insurance)?
  2. What are we most afraid of losing or having go wrong?
  3. What do we want this family to be known for in 50 years?
  4. Who would we trust to be trustee? Who is the backup?
  5. Which causes should the family foundation support?
  6. Who wants to work in a family business, and who prefers to own, invest, or give?
  7. How will we make decisions when we disagree?
  8. What does each age group need to learn so everyone is ready?
  9. Which state are we in, and who are our attorney and CPA?

SECTION 9 Raising the Next Generation

Rules, expectations, and earning access, so what the family built is not lost

You can build the best trust, LLCs, and foundation in the world and still lose everything, if the people who come after you never learn how to handle it. Money is a tool. In untrained hands it disappears fast. This section is for the children, grandchildren, and everyone who comes after: this was built for you, and it was built with rules, because the rules are what keep it alive.

Why the Next Generation Has to Learn

There is an old saying: “shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations.” It means the first generation works with their sleeves rolled up and builds the wealth. The second generation watches them and learns some of it. The third generation only sees the results, never learns the work, and often spends it all. Back to shirtsleeves.

  • Money without knowledge doesn’t last. The wealth lasts only as long as the skills, habits, and values behind it.
  • Surprise is dangerous. A young person who suddenly receives a large amount, with no training and no rules, is more likely to overspend, get taken advantage of, or fall into fights with relatives.
  • Everyone is a steward. A steward takes care of something that belongs to many people over a long time. You are not just receiving the family’s money. You are taking over the job of protecting it for the people who come after you.

The Power Scene: Rules for Accessing the Trust

From the show Power: Ghost’s will

In the scene, the will of James “Ghost” St. Patrick is read. It lays out who gets what, and it shows that money can come with rules:

  • Tasha (his wife): receives nothing from the estate itself, but her rent is covered out of the trust for one year.
  • The children: the estate is split in thirds among Yasmin, Tariq, and Raina. Because Raina has passed away, her share goes to Tariq, so Yasmin gets one-third and Tariq gets two-thirds.
  • What is included: property, shares in the nightclubs (including Truth), and a watch collection. The life insurance, stocks, and bonds go straight to the children, and Tasha has no access to them.
  • The condition for Tariq: none of his money is handed over right away. It is held in a trust until he completes a four-year university degree with at least a 3.5 GPA. If he doesn’t meet the conditions, he receives nothing.
What the scene teaches
  • Held, not handed over. Putting the money in a trust means someone besides the young person controls when and how they get to it.
  • Rules turn money into a reason to grow. The condition (finish school and do it well) pushes Tariq toward something that will help him for life, with or without the money.
  • The rules are clear. Four years. 3.5 GPA. No guessing, so no arguing about what was meant.
  • Different people, different support. Tasha gets short-term help (a year of rent) so she isn’t put out on the street, but not ownership of the estate.
  • The grantor’s wishes lead. The rulebook was written by the person who built the wealth, and it keeps working after he is gone. That is the power of a trust.
Reality check: TV vs. real planning
  • The scene is an all-or-nothing rule: meet the condition, or get zero. Real planners usually build in more flexibility, because life happens. A serious illness, a family emergency, or a learning difference can stop someone from hitting an exact number, even if they are working hard.
  • Conditions must be legal and reasonable. A court can refuse to enforce a rule that is against the law or against public policy, so an attorney should write them.
  • Rules work best when they are known early, so the young person can prepare and doesn’t feel ambushed by a will being read after a death. The best version is a family that has talked about it for years.
  • Rules without teaching can turn into resentment. Rules with teaching, mentoring, and love turn into a path.

Types of Rules a Trust Can Use

  • Age milestones (staged distributions): small portions at 25, 30, and 35 instead of everything at once.
  • Incentive rules: money is released when the person reaches a goal: finishing a degree or trade certificate, holding a steady job, or running a business for a set time. The Ghost scene’s GPA rule is one example of this.
  • Matching rules: the trust adds money to what the person earns or saves themselves. This rewards work instead of waiting.
  • Pay-direct rules: tuition goes straight to the school, and a home down payment goes straight to the closing. The money never passes through hands that could waste it.
  • Discretionary distributions: the trustee decides, case by case, whether a request fits the trust’s purpose. A common guide is HEMS: Health, Education, Maintenance, and Support.
  • Project proposals: to fund a business idea, the person writes a plan and presents it to the family council, just like asking an investor.
  • Protections: spendthrift clauses, and keeping trust money in the beneficiary’s own name rather than mixed with others, so it is harder for creditors or a bad deal to reach it. (Rules vary by state.)

Sample Buford access rules (for discussion, not legal drafting)
  • Education: tuition and fees are paid straight to the school while the student is enrolled and making progress. The family chooses a standard, such as a degree with a minimum GPA, or a trade certificate, or two years of running a real business.
  • First home: the trust helps with a down payment once the person has a steady income and a savings record.
  • Milestone shares: a portion at 25, 30, and 35, each tied to staying on track.
  • Matching: the trust adds money for every dollar the person earns and invests, up to a limit.
  • Business ideas: a written plan, a presentation to the family council, and a vote.
  • Hardship: health or true emergencies are handled by the trustee’s judgment.
  • Always: requests are made in writing, answered in writing, and recorded by the family office.

How to Ask for Money From the Trust

  1. Write the request. What you need, how much, and why.
  2. Show how it fits the rules. Which rule or purpose does it match?
  3. Give a plan. How will it be used, and what is the result?
  4. The trustee (and council, if needed) reviews it.
  5. Decision in writing. A yes, a no, or a “not yet, here’s what to do.”
  6. Report back. Show how the money was used.
The Buford Family Promise (sample, to discuss as a family)
  • We learn before we receive.
  • We tell the truth about money.
  • We follow the rules, even when no one is watching.
  • We never co-sign, lend, or gamble away what the family built.
  • We help the next person come up.
  • We remember this was built for everyone who comes after us.
